Before relativity: what an inertial frame is, and the symmetries of Newtonian mechanics that Einstein's theory replaces.

Galilei transformation
\[ \vec r^{\,\prime} = R\vec r - \vec v_0t + \vec r_0, \qquad t' = t + t_0 \]

A rotation \(R\), a boost \(\vec v_0\), and translations \(\vec r_0\), \(t_0\). Time is absolute, and velocities simply add.

An event is a position \(\vec r\) together with a time \(t\). An inertial frame is one in which a free particle moves with constant velocity. The Galilei transformations relate inertial frames in classical mechanics: they leave accelerations unchanged up to the rotation, so Newton's second law takes the same form in each frame.

Einstein summation convention
\[ v_iw_i \equiv \sum_{i}v_iw_i \]

A repeated index is summed over. Each index may appear at most twice in a term.

Rules

  • Free indices appear once in a term, and must match across every term of an equation.
  • Summation (dummy) indices appear exactly twice, and can be renamed freely.
  • \(\delta_{ij}\) is 1 if \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise, so \(v_j\delta_{ji} = v_i\) and \(\delta_{ii} = 3\) in three dimensions.

Orthogonal matrices

\(R\) is orthogonal if \(RR^T = 1 = R^TR\). Then \(\det R = \pm1\), \(R^{-1} = R^T\), and \((R\vec v)\cdot(R\vec w) = \vec v\cdot\vec w\). In index notation:

\[ R_{ik}R_{jk} = \delta_{ij}. \]
Checking covariance. \(M_{ij}v_j = w_j\) is wrong: \(i\) is free on the left but absent on the right, and \(j\) appears three times. In Minkowski space, a summation index also needs one upper and one lower position.

The principle of relativity and causality leave only Lorentz or Galilei transformations. The speed of light is the tie breaker.

First postulate: the principle of relativity

The laws of nature are the same in all inertial frames.

Second postulate: the speed of light

The speed of light in a vacuum is the same in all inertial frames, and independent of the motion of the source.

Lorentz transformation, standard configuration
\begin{equation} x' = \gamma(x - vt), \quad y' = y, \quad z' = z, \quad t' = \gamma\!\left(t - \frac{v}{c^2}x\right) \tag{105} \end{equation} \[ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \]

Frame \(I'\) moves at velocity \((v,0,0)\) relative to \(I\). For the inverse, swap primed and unprimed and set \(v \to -v\).

Rapidity
\[ ct' = ct\cosh\phi - x\sinh\phi, \qquad x' = -ct\sinh\phi + x\cosh\phi, \qquad \frac vc = \tanh\phi \]

A hyperbolic rotation. Collinear boosts add their rapidities: \(\phi = \phi_1 + \phi_2\).

Addition of collinear velocities
\begin{equation} v_{12} = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{1 + v_1v_2/c^2} \tag{108} \end{equation}

The result never exceeds \(c\), and \(v_1 = c\) gives \(v_{12} = c\).

Derivation in outline

  1. Affine. The principle of relativity implies that space and time are homogeneous and isotropic, so the transformation of a separation depends only on the separation. Hence the transformation is affine, and linear for frames in standard configuration.
  2. \(y\) and \(z\). \(z = 0\) implies \(z' = 0\), so \(z' = C(v)z\). Isotropy makes \(C\) even, and the inverse transformation gives \(C(v)^2 = 1\). Setting \(v = 0\) gives \(C = 1\), so \(z' = z\), and likewise \(y' = y\).
  3. \(x\). \(x' = 0\) for \(x = vt\), so \(x' = \gamma(v)(x - vt)\), with \(\gamma\) even.
  4. \(t\). Combining with the inverse \(x = \gamma(x' + vt')\), and isotropy, gives \(t' = \gamma\left(t - \dfrac{vx}{\eta}\right)\) and \(\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/\eta}}\), where \(\eta(v)\) is an even function.
  5. Group property. Two successive transformations must form a single one of the same type. Comparing coefficients gives \(\eta = \text{const.}\), and the addition law for collinear velocities.
  6. Causality. For \(\eta \lt 0\) the temporal order of any two events could be reversed, so \(\eta \gt 0\). This leaves \(\eta = c^2\), the Lorentz transformations, or \(\eta = \infty\), the Galilei transformations.
  7. Second postulate. It acts as a tie breaker between the two, and identifies the parameter \(c\) with the speed of light.

Consequences

  • No signal can travel faster than \(c\), given causality, so rigid bodies and incompressible fluids are impossible.
  • Recession velocities can exceed \(c\), as they involve no exchange of matter, energy or information. An example is the spot of a rotating laser on a distant screen.
  • Classical mechanics holds approximately for \(|\vec v| \ll c\), that is \(T \ll mc^2\).

Times and distances depend on the observer, but one combination does not.

Invariant line element
\begin{equation} ds^2 = c^2dt^2 - dx^2 - dy^2 - dz^2 \tag{111} \end{equation}

The relative minus sign is essential. \(ds^2\) can be zero for distinct events, and it can be negative.

The Lorentz transformations are linear, so intervals transform like the coordinates:

\begin{equation} \Delta t' = \gamma\!\left(\Delta t - \frac{v}{c^2}\Delta x\right), \qquad \Delta x' = \gamma(\Delta x - v\Delta t), \qquad \Delta y' = \Delta y, \qquad \Delta z' = \Delta z. \tag{109} \end{equation}

Moving clocks run slow and moving rods shorten. Both effects are symmetric between observers, and both follow from the Lorentz transformation.

Time dilation
\begin{equation} \Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta\tau = \left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{-1/2}\Delta\tau \gt \Delta\tau \tag{114} \end{equation}

\(\Delta\tau\) is the proper time, the time measured in the rest frame of the clock. "Moving clocks go slower".

Length contraction
\[ L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} \]

\(L_0\) is the proper length, measured in the rest frame of the body. In a frame where the body moves, both ends must be measured at the same time.

Relativity of simultaneity

Two events with \(\Delta t = 0\) and \(\Delta x \neq 0\) have \(\Delta t' = -\gamma v\Delta x/c^2 \neq 0\). Events that are simultaneous in one frame are not in another. This resolves the "paradoxes" below.

ParadoxThe pole in the barn

A man runs at \(v = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}c\) towards a barn \(3\,\text{m}\) deep, carrying a pole of proper length \(L_0 = 6\,\text{m}\). At \(t = 0\) the front of the pole hits the back of the barn, and at the same time the door closes. Is the end of the pole inside the barn when the door closes?

Barn frame: yes. Here \(\gamma = 2\), so \(L = \tfrac12L_0 = 3\,\text{m}\), and the pole just fits.

Pole frame: yes. The barn has depth \(1.5\,\text{m}\), which naively cannot hold a \(6\,\text{m}\) pole. But "door closes" is at \(x = -3\,\text{m}\), \(t = 0\) in the barn frame, which transforms to \(x' = -6\,\text{m}\), \(t' = 3\sqrt3\,\text{m}/c\). That is later than "pole touches the back of the barn" at \(t' = 0\), and \(x' = -6\,\text{m}\) is the end of the pole.

The answer is yes in both frames, for different reasons: Lorentz contraction in one, and the fact that simultaneity is not absolute in the other. It is counter-intuitive, but not paradoxical.

ParadoxThe twins

Twin 1 stays at home and twin 2 makes a round trip. Proper time is path dependent:

\[ \Delta\tau_{[2]} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\,\sqrt{1 - \frac{v(t)^2}{c^2}} \lt \int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt = \Delta\tau_{[1]}. \]

So the twin making the round trip is younger at their reunion. The roles cannot be exchanged, because there is an absolute difference: the twin on Earth remains in an inertial frame, while the travelling twin does not, and experiences acceleration.

The frequency of light depends on the motion of source and receiver. Unlike the classical effect, it includes time dilation.

Relativistic Doppler effect
\[ \nu_R = \left(1 + \frac{v_r}{c}\right)^{-1}\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{1/2}\nu_S \]

\(v_r\) is the radial velocity of the source, positive when moving away from the receiver.

CaseFormulaEffect
Longitudinal\(\nu_R = \sqrt{\dfrac{1 - v/c}{1 + v/c}}\;\nu_S\)\(v \gt 0\): redshift. \(v \lt 0\): blueshift.
Transverse\(\nu_R = \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\;\nu_S\)Always a redshift, from time dilation alone.

The transverse effect is purely relativistic, and gave the first experimental verification of time dilation (Ives and Stillwell, 1938).

Derivation in outline

  1. The source period \(\delta t = 1/\nu_S\) is dilated in the receiver's frame to \(\delta t' = \gamma\,\delta t\).
  2. During one period, the distance to the source grows by \(v_r\delta t'\), adding a travel time \(v_r\delta t'/c\).
  3. So the receiver sees \(\delta t_R = (1 + v_r/c)\gamma\,\delta t\), and \(\nu_R = 1/\delta t_R\).

Use the substitution \(u = 2\sinh s\) to evaluate \(J(x) = \displaystyle\int_0^x\frac{du}{\sqrt{4 + u^2}}\). Write the answer in terms of a logarithm.

Show solution
  1. \(du = 2\cosh s\,ds\), and \(\sqrt{4 + u^2} = 2\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2s} = 2\cosh s\).
  2. So the integrand reduces to \(ds\), and \(J = s = \operatorname{arsinh}(x/2)\).
  3. Using \(\operatorname{arsinh}w = \ln\big(w + \sqrt{w^2 + 1}\big)\): \[ J(x) = \ln\left(\frac x2 + \sqrt{\frac{x^2}{4} + 1}\right). \]

Expand \(f(v) = \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\) for \(v \ll c\), up to order \(v^4/c^4\). A clock moves at \(v = 0.01c\). By what fraction does it run slow?

Show solution
  1. Using \((1 + x)^{1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12x - \tfrac18x^2 + \dots\) with \(x = -v^2/c^2\): \[ f(v) = 1 - \frac{v^2}{2c^2} - \frac{v^4}{8c^4} + \dots \]
  2. The moving clock's rate is \(d\tau/dt = 1/\gamma = f(v)\).
  3. So the fractional slowing is \(\dfrac{v^2}{2c^2} = 5\times10^{-5}\).

Show that if \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) are orthogonal, so is \(R_1R_2\). Show that \(R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}\) is orthogonal, with \(\det R = 1\). Why does \(\det R = \pm1\) imply that \(R\) is invertible?

Show solution
  1. \((R_1R_2)(R_1R_2)^T = R_1R_2R_2^TR_1^T = R_1R_1^T = 1\). ✓
  2. \(R(\theta)R(\theta)^T\) has diagonal entries \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), and off-diagonal entries \(\cos\theta\sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0\). ✓
  3. \(\det R = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\).
  4. A square matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is non-zero. Here the inverse is \(R^T\).

Under \(\vec r^{\,\prime} = R\vec r - \vec v_0t + \vec r_0\), \(t' = t + t_0\), find how the velocity and acceleration of a particle transform. Hence show that Newton's second law keeps its form, if \(\vec F' = R\vec F\).

Show solution
  1. \(dt' = dt\), and \(R\), \(\vec v_0\), \(\vec r_0\) are constant.
  2. Differentiating once: \(\vec v^{\,\prime} = R\vec v - \vec v_0\). So velocities shift by \(\vec v_0\), after rotating.
  3. Differentiating again: \(\vec a^{\,\prime} = R\vec a\). The boost and translations disappear.
  4. So \(m\vec a^{\,\prime} = Rm\vec a = R\vec F = \vec F'\), the same form as in the original frame.

Show directly that \(c^2dt^2 - dx^2\) is invariant under the standard Lorentz transformation.

Show solution
  1. \(c\,dt' = \gamma(c\,dt - \beta\,dx)\) and \(dx' = \gamma(dx - \beta c\,dt)\), with \(\beta = v/c\).
  2. Squaring and subtracting, the cross terms \(\mp2\gamma^2\beta c\,dt\,dx\) cancel: \[ c^2dt'^2 - dx'^2 = \gamma^2(1 - \beta^2)(c^2dt^2 - dx^2). \]
  3. Since \(\gamma^2(1 - \beta^2) = 1\), this equals \(c^2dt^2 - dx^2\). ✓

Show solution

  2. For rapidities, \(e^{2\phi} = \dfrac{1 + \tanh\phi}{1 - \tanh\phi}\). So \(e^{2\phi_1} = \dfrac{1.6}{0.4} = 4\) and \(e^{2\phi_2} = \dfrac{1.8}{0.2} = 9\).

A spaceship leaves Earth at constant velocity. After 4 years by its own clocks, it reaches a star and sends a radio signal. The signal reaches Earth 16 years after departure, by Earth clocks. Find the ship's speed, the distance to the star, and the signal's travel time.

Show solution
  1. In Earth's frame, the ship arrives at \(t = 4\gamma\) years, at distance \(D = vt\).
  2. The signal takes \(D/c = \beta t\), so \(4\gamma(1 + \beta) = 16\).
  3. Since \(\gamma(1 + \beta) = \sqrt{\dfrac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}\), we get \(\dfrac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta} = 16\), so \(\beta = \dfrac{15}{17}\).
  4. Then \(\gamma = \dfrac{17}{8}\), so \(t = 8.5\) years, and \(D = \beta ct = 7.5\) light years.
  5. The signal takes 7.5 years, which gives \(8.5 + 7.5 = 16\). ✓

Show that, to first order in \(v/c\), an observer measures \(u \approx \dfrac cn + v\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{n^2}\right)\). Check the case \(n = 1\). Compare with the exact result for \(n = 1.5\), \(v = 0.1c\).

Show solution
  1. Velocity addition gives \(u = \dfrac{c/n + v}{1 + v/(nc)}\).
  2. Expanding \(\big(1 + v/(nc)\big)^{-1} \approx 1 - v/(nc)\): \[ u \approx \left(\frac cn + v\right)\left(1 - \frac{v}{nc}\right) \approx \frac cn + v - \frac{v}{n^2}. \]
  3. For \(n = 1\), \(u = c\), as the second postulate requires.
  4. For \(n = 1.5\), \(v = 0.1c\): the approximation gives \(0.722c\), and the exact formula gives \(\dfrac{0.7667}{1.0667}c = 0.719c\).

Muons have a mean lifetime of \(2.2\,\mu\text{s}\) in their rest frame. How far does a muon with \(v = 0.995c\) travel in one lifetime, measured in Earth's frame? Show that an observer moving with the muon agrees that it reaches the ground from \(6\,\text{km}\) up, using length contraction.

Show solution
  1. \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.995^2} \approx 10.0\).
  2. In Earth's frame, the lifetime is dilated to \(\gamma\tau \approx 22\,\mu\text{s}\), so the range is \(v\gamma\tau \approx 6.6\,\text{km}\).
  3. In the muon's frame, the \(6\,\text{km}\) is contracted to \(6/\gamma \approx 0.6\,\text{km}\).
  4. In one lifetime, the ground approaches by \(v\tau \approx 0.66\,\text{km}\), which is more than \(0.6\,\text{km}\). Both frames agree that it arrives.

A pole of rest length \(20\,\text{m}\) moves through a barn of length \(10\,\text{m}\). What speed makes it just fit, in the barn's frame? In the pole's frame, what is the time between the two doors shutting?

Show solution
  1. Fitting requires \(20/\gamma = 10\), so \(\gamma = 2\) and \(\beta = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 0.866\).
  2. In the barn's frame, the door events have \(\Delta t = 0\) and \(\Delta x = 10\,\text{m}\).
  3. In the pole's frame: \[ c\Delta t' = \gamma(c\Delta t - \beta\Delta x) = -2(0.866)(10\,\text{m}) \approx -17.3\,\text{m}. \]
  4. So the far door shuts first, by \(\Delta t' \approx 58\,\text{ns}\). The two doors are not simultaneous in this frame, which resolves the paradox.

A spectral line emitted at \(656\,\text{nm}\) is observed from a receding galaxy at \(700\,\text{nm}\). Find the galaxy's speed. What wavelength would be seen if the same speed were entirely transverse?

Show solution
  1. Since \(\lambda \propto 1/\nu\), \(\dfrac{\lambda_R}{\lambda_S} = \sqrt{\dfrac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}\). The ratio is \(700/656 = 1.0671\).
  2. Squaring gives \(\dfrac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta} = 1.1387\), so \(\beta = \dfrac{0.1387}{2.1387} \approx 0.065\).
  3. For transverse motion, \(\lambda_R = \gamma\lambda_S\).
  4. With \(\gamma \approx 1.0021\), \(\lambda_R \approx 657.4\,\text{nm}\), a far smaller shift.

Space and time join into one four-dimensional geometry, with a scalar product that is not positive definite.

Minkowski scalar product
\[ x\cdot y = \eta_{\mu\nu}x^\mu y^\nu = x^0y^0 - x^1y^1 - x^2y^2 - x^3y^3, \qquad \eta = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1) \]

With \(x^\mu = (x^0, \vec x) = (ct, \vec x)\). It is invariant under Lorentz transformations.

Raising and lowering

A four-vector has contravariant components \(A^\mu\) that transform as \(A'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu\). The metric lowers indices, and its inverse \(\eta^{\mu\nu}\) raises them:

\[ A_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\nu = (A^0, -\vec A), \qquad A^\mu = \eta^{\mu\nu}A_\nu, \qquad A\cdot B = A^\mu B_\mu. \]

In a valid covariant equation, every free index matches in height and name, and each summation pair has one upper and one lower index.

The sign of \(x^2\) tells you whether one event can influence another, and every observer agrees on it.

Using standard forms. Any one vector can be brought to its standard form by a Lorentz transformation, which makes proofs short. Two vectors usually cannot be brought to standard form simultaneously.

A future-pointing vector cannot be turned into a past-pointing one by a Lorentz transformation, so causal past and future are invariant. For spacelike-separated events there is always a frame where they are simultaneous, and frames with either order. This is consistent with causality, since such events cannot influence each other.

The length of a timelike curve is the time measured by a clock carried along it.

Proper time along a worldline
\[ \tau = \frac1c\int\sqrt{ds^2} = \int\sqrt{1 - \frac{v(t)^2}{c^2}}\;dt \]

Proper time depends on the path, and is a Lorentz invariant. The straight worldline between two events has the longest proper time.

At each moment, an accelerated clock can be compared with its momentarily co-moving reference frame (MCRF): the inertial frame in which it is instantaneously at rest. Between infinitesimally close moments, \(dt = \gamma\,d\tau\), where now \(\gamma\) depends on time.

All Lorentz transformations are captured by one matrix condition: they preserve the Minkowski metric.

Lorentz group \(O(1,3)\)
\[ \Lambda^T\eta\Lambda = \eta \]

Taking determinants gives \(\det\Lambda = \pm1\). The \(00\) component gives \((\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 \geq 1\).

So \(x'^0 = \gamma(x^0 - \vec v\cdot\vec x)\), as used on Sheet 4. Use this when frames are not in standard configuration. The \(\Lambda^i{}_j\) block is the standard result, and is not printed in the notes.

Poincaré group

Adding translations gives \(x'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + a^\mu\). This is the group \(IO(1,3)\), the largest group relating inertial frames that preserves the speed of light.

4-velocity
\begin{equation} U^\mu := \frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}, \qquad (U^\mu) = (\gamma, \gamma\vec v), \qquad U_\mu U^\mu = 1 \tag{121} \end{equation}

In units with \(c = 1\). Keeping \(c\): \(U = \gamma(c, \vec v)\) and \(U^2 = c^2\). It is timelike, and is the unit tangent vector of the worldline.

4-acceleration
\[ A^\mu = \frac{dU^\mu}{d\tau}, \qquad A^\mu U_\mu = 0, \qquad A^\mu A_\mu \leq 0 \]

In the MCRF, \(A = (0, \vec a)\). The proper acceleration is \(\alpha = |\vec a_{\text{MCRF}}|\).

In a general frame, with \(c = 1\):

\begin{equation} A = \gamma(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma\vec v + \gamma\vec a), \qquad \dot\gamma = (1 - v^2)^{-3/2}\,\vec v\cdot\vec a. \tag{122} \end{equation}

The orthogonality has a quick proof: differentiating \(U^\mu U_\mu = 1\) gives \(2U^\mu A_\mu = 0\).

Hyperbolic motion, starting from rest
\[ \gamma v = \alpha t, \qquad x = \frac{c^2}{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{1 + \frac{\alpha^2t^2}{c^2}} - 1\right), \qquad \tau = \frac c\alpha\sinh^{-1}\frac{\alpha t}{c} \]

For motion along \(x\), \(\alpha = \gamma^3\,dv/dt = \dfrac{d}{dt}(\gamma v)\).

Energy and momentum are two parts of one four-vector, whose length is the invariant mass.

4-momentum and energy
\[ p^\mu = mU^\mu, \qquad \vec p = \gamma m\vec v \] \begin{equation} E = p^0c = \frac{mc^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \tag{127} \end{equation}
Energy–momentum relation
\[ p_\mu p^\mu = m^2c^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E^2 = m^2c^4 + \vec p^{\,2}c^2 \]

\(m\) always means the Lorentz-invariant rest mass.

Equation of motion

The relativistic second law is below, with \(K^\mu\) the relativistic force. It applies to any force, but not gravity.

\begin{equation} \frac{d}{d\tau}p^\mu = m\frac{d}{d\tau}U^\mu = K^\mu \tag{126} \end{equation}

Writing \(\vec F := \gamma^{-1}\vec K\), in units with \(c = 1\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}(\gamma m\vec v) = \vec F, \qquad K^0 = \gamma\vec F\cdot\vec v, \qquad \vec F\cdot\vec v = \frac{d}{dt}(m\gamma) = \frac{d}{dt}p^0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}(E\vec v) = \vec F. \]

So the inertia of a particle is determined by its total energy, and not by its rest mass.

Low-velocity expansion

\[ E = mc^2 + \tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac38m\frac{v^4}{c^2} + \dots \]

The first term is the rest energy \(E_0 = mc^2\), and the rest is kinetic. As \(v \to c\), \(E \to \infty\), so \(c\) can only be approached.

Massless particles

Massless particles
\[ E = |\vec p|c, \qquad v = c, \qquad p_\mu p^\mu = 0 \]

There is no rest frame, so no proper time and no \(U^\mu\). Use an affine parameter: \(p^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda\).

With quantum mechanics, \(E = h\nu\) and \(\vec p = h\vec k\), with \(|\vec k| = \lambda^{-1}\). Worldlines of photons are lightlike lines in Minkowski space.

Conservation of 4-momentum, plus the invariance of \(p^2\), solves almost every particle problem. Units with \(c = 1\) from here.

Conservation
\[ P^\mu_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^Np_i^\mu = \text{const.} \]

For a closed system, with no external force. The invariant energy, or invariant mass, \(E_{\text{inv}}^2 := P^\mu_{\text{total}}P_{\text{total}\,\mu}\), is both conserved and Lorentz invariant.

In the CMS (center of mass system), \(\vec P_{\text{total}} = \vec0\), and the total energy equals \(E_{\text{inv}}\). In any other frame the total energy is larger. The invariant mass is the maximal energy available to create new particles in a scattering process.

Method

  1. Write down each 4-momentum in a convenient frame.
  2. Isolate the unknown you do not want, then square, so that it disappears through \(p^2 = m^2\).
  3. Use invariants, evaluating each scalar product in whichever frame is easiest.

Two-body decay at rest

For \(M \to m_1 + m_2\) in the CMS, \(M = E_1 + E_2 = m_1 + m_2 + E_{\text{kin},1} + E_{\text{kin},2}\), so a decay is impossible if \(M \lt m_1 + m_2\). If \(M \gt m_1 + m_2\), momentum and energy conservation give

\[ E_1 = \frac{M^2 + m_1^2 - m_2^2}{2M}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{M^2 + m_2^2 - m_1^2}{2M}. \]

The energies are completely determined by the masses. This is special to \(1 \to 2\) decays.

Threshold energy

For \(1 + 2 \to 3 + 4\), the minimal energy in the CMS just provides the masses of the final state particles, \(E_{\min}^2 = (m_3 + m_4)^2\). In the Lab frame, particle 2 is at rest, and a prime marks Lab quantities: \(E_{\text{inv}}'^2 = m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2E_1'm_2\). As \(E_{\text{inv}}\) is Lorentz invariant, the threshold energy in the Lab frame is

\[ E_{1\min}' = \frac{(m_3 + m_4)^2 - m_1^2 - m_2^2}{2m_2}. \]

The CMS moves at \(\beta = |\vec p_{\text{tot}}|/E_{\text{tot}}\) in the lab.

Compton scattering

Compton scattering, \(\gamma + e \to \gamma' + e'\)
\begin{equation} \frac{E - E'}{EE'} = \frac{1}{E'} - \frac1E = \frac1m(1 - \cos\theta) \tag{131} \end{equation} \[ \frac ch(\lambda' - \lambda) = \frac1m(1 - \cos\theta) \]

A photon of energy \(E\) scatters off an electron at rest in the Lab frame, leaving with energy \(E'\) at angle \(\theta\). The change in wavelength is determined by the scattering angle alone.

With 4-vectors: \(p + q = p' + q'\) gives \(p'^2 = (p + q - q')^2\). Using \(q^2 = q'^2 = 0\) and \(p^2 = p'^2 = m^2\), this becomes \(p(q - q') - qq' = 0\). In the rest frame of the initial electron, \(p = (m, \vec0)\), \(q = E(1, 1, 0, 0)\) and \(q' = E'(1, \cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)\), so \(EE'(1 - \cos\theta) = m(E - E')\).

Inverse Compton scattering. Off an ultrarelativistic particle with \(E_e = \gamma m\), \(\gamma \gg 1\):

\[ \frac{E'}{E} = \frac{4\gamma^2}{1 + 4\dfrac{\gamma E}{m}}. \]

For \(E \ll \gamma m\), the scattered photon gains energy, \(E' \gg E\).

Newtonian gravity acts instantly, which clashes with special relativity. The way out begins with a simple fact: everything falls the same way.

(Strong) equivalence principle

In a gravitational field one can choose, at any space-time point, a locally inertial frame (LIF), such that the laws of nature are those of an unaccelerated frame in absence of gravity. Thus in a LIF the laws of nature take the form known from special relativity.

\[ g_{\mu\nu}(P) = \eta_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(P) = 0 \]

In coordinates, a LIF at \(P\) is a choice of Riemannian normal coordinates. The second derivatives of the metric cannot be set to zero in general.

Newtonian gravity

The field is \(\vec G = -\nabla\phi\), and the force on a probe is \(\vec F = m\vec G = -m\nabla\phi\). The potential of a mass density \(\rho\) is determined by the Poisson equation

\begin{equation} \Delta\phi = 4\pi G_N\rho. \tag{133} \end{equation}

Two problems. Newton's theory assumes that gravity acts instantaneously, which conflicts with a maximal speed for the propagation of energy. And inertial and gravitational mass are equal with very high precision, with no logical reason why this must be the case.

Consequences

  • Elevator thought experiment. A homogeneous gravitational field cannot be distinguished from a state of constant acceleration in absence of gravity. A freely falling elevator in a homogeneous gravitational field cannot be distinguished from a freely falling spacecraft in empty space.
  • Only local inertial frames. When gravity is relevant, we cannot assume the existence of global inertial frames, but only of locally inertial frames.
  • General covariance. The Lorentz transformations lose their privileged role, so the laws of nature must be covariant with respect to general coordinate transformations.
  • Gravitational time dilation. Signals sent from a height \(H\) above an observer, with time difference \(\delta t_A\), arrive with \(\delta t_B \simeq \left(1 - \dfrac{Hg}{c^2}\right)\delta t_A\). The lower clock runs slower.

A \(d\)-dimensional manifold looks locally like \(\mathbb R^d\). The metric supplies distances, and tells you how to raise and lower indices.

Tensors

Tensor transformation laws
\[ A^{\bar\mu} = J^{\bar\mu}{}_\mu A^\mu, \qquad A_{\bar\mu} = J^\mu{}_{\bar\mu}A_\mu, \qquad J^{\bar\mu}{}_\mu = \frac{\partial x^{\bar\mu}}{\partial x^\mu} \]

Each upper index gets one Jacobian, and each lower index gets one inverse Jacobian. A tensor of type \((m,n)\) has \(m\) upper and \(n\) lower indices.

  • A tensor equation that holds in one coordinate system holds in all of them.
  • The gradient \(\partial_\mu\phi\) of a scalar is a covariant vector.
  • \(A_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}A^\nu\) and \(A^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}A_\nu\).
  • Contracting an upper and a lower index gives a tensor with two fewer indices.
  • Quotient theorem: if \(A^\lambda P_{\lambda\mu_1\cdots}\) is a tensor for all vectors \(A^\lambda\), then \(P_{\lambda\mu_1\cdots}\) is a tensor.

On a curved space, vectors at different points cannot be compared directly. Parallel transport says how to carry one along a curve.

Christoffel symbols
\[ \Gamma_{\mu\nu\rho} = \tfrac12\big(\partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} + \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}\big), \qquad \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} = g^{\lambda\rho}\Gamma_{\rho\mu\nu} \]

The first and second kinds. Both are symmetric in the last two indices, and \(\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\mu\nu\rho} + \Gamma_{\nu\mu\rho}\). They do not form a tensor: they can be zero in one coordinate system while being non-zero in others.

Parallel transport
\[ dA^\lambda = -\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\rho}A^\mu dx^\rho, \qquad \frac{dA^\lambda}{dt} + \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}A^\mu\frac{dx^\nu}{dt} = 0 \tag{137} \]

The second form is for a finite curve. For a covariant vector, \(dA_\nu = \Gamma_{\mu\nu\rho}A^\mu dx^\rho\), which is equation (135).

The scalar product is invariant under parallel transport: \(d(A^\nu B_\nu) = 0\). The result of a parallel transport does not only depend on the endpoints, but on the choice of the curve itself.

The Christoffel symbols transform with an extra, non-tensor term:

ExamplePolar coordinates on the plane

With \(ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\phi^2\), the only non-zero derivative is \(\partial_rg_{\phi\phi} = 2r\). The non-zero symbols are

\[ \Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi} = -r, \qquad \Gamma^\phi{}_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi{}_{\phi r} = \frac1r. \]

They are non-zero even though the plane is flat. In Cartesian coordinates, they all vanish. Transport gives \(dA^r = rA^\phi d\phi\) and \(dA^\phi = -\tfrac1r(A^rd\phi + A^\phi dr)\). The components change only because the polar basis depends on position.

Straight lines generalised: curves whose tangent stays parallel to itself. Free particles follow them.

Geodesic equation
\begin{equation} \frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\tau^2} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\rho}{d\tau} = 0 \tag{139} \end{equation}

Valid only for an affine parameter, for which \(g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu\) is constant. Affine parameters are related by \(\tau \to a\tau + b\).

\[ \partial_0g_{\mu\nu} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_0, \; p_0 = mu_0 \text{ conserved along a geodesic} \]

ExampleGeodesics on the sphere

For \(ds^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)\), the non-zero symbols are \(\Gamma^\theta{}_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta\) and \(\Gamma^\phi{}_{\theta\phi} = \cot\theta\). The geodesic equations are

\[ \ddot\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = 0, \qquad \ddot\phi + 2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi = 0. \]

Partial derivatives of tensors are not tensors. Subtracting off the parallel-transported vector repairs this.

Covariant derivatives
\[ \nabla_\nu A^\mu = \partial_\nu A^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\sigma}A^\sigma, \qquad \nabla_\nu A_\mu = \partial_\nu A_\mu - \Gamma^\sigma{}_{\mu\nu}A_\sigma \]

One \(+\Gamma\) for each upper index, and one \(-\Gamma\) for each lower index. A \((m,n)\) tensor becomes a \((m,n+1)\) tensor.

Metric compatibility
\[ \nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu} = 0 \]

So contraction and covariant differentiation commute, and indices can be raised and lowered freely under \(\nabla\).

General rule

\[ \nabla_\sigma T^\rho{}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\sigma T^\rho{}_{\mu\nu} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\sigma\lambda}T^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\sigma\mu}T^\rho{}_{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\sigma\nu}T^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}. \]
  • On a scalar, \(\nabla_\mu\phi = \partial_\mu\phi\).
  • \(\nabla\) obeys the product rule.
  • For higher derivatives, work from the outside in: \(\nabla_\mu(\nabla_\nu A^\rho)\) treats \(\nabla_\nu A^\rho\) as a \((1,1)\) tensor.
  • This \(\nabla\), the Levi-Civita connection, is the unique one that is metric compatible and torsion free.
Quick proof of \(\nabla g = 0\). In local inertial coordinates at \(P\), \(\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(P) = 0\) and \(\Gamma(P) = 0\), so \(\nabla_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(P) = 0\). It is a tensor, so this holds in all coordinates, and \(P\) is arbitrary.

Curvature is what cannot be removed by a change of coordinates, and one tensor captures all of it.

Riemann curvature tensor
\[ R^\mu{}_{\nu\rho\sigma} = \partial_\rho\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\sigma\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho} + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\lambda\rho}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\rho} \]

A space is flat if and only if \(R^\mu{}_{\nu\rho\sigma} = 0\).

It measures two equivalent things. Parallel transport round an infinitesimal parallelogram changes a vector by \(\Delta A^\mu = R^\mu{}_{\nu\rho\sigma}A^\nu d_1x^\rho d_2x^\sigma\). And covariant derivatives fail to commute, by the Ricci identity:

\[ \nabla_\rho\nabla_\nu A_\mu - \nabla_\nu\nabla_\rho A_\mu = R^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu\rho}A_\lambda. \]

"Space-time tells matter how to move." Free particles follow geodesics, and Newton's gravity appears when fields are weak and motion is slow.

Equation of motion in curved space-time
\[ m\big(\ddot x^\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\big) = K^\lambda \]

\(K^\lambda\) contains non-gravitational forces only. Gravity is entirely in the metric. Massless particles follow null geodesics.

This follows from the equivalence principle: in a LIF, \(m\,d^2\xi^\alpha/d\tau^2 = 0\). Transforming to general coordinates generates the \(\Gamma\) term.

Newtonian limit
\[ g_{00} \approx 1 + \frac{2\phi}{c^2} \]

For slow motion, a weak field \(g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}\), and a static metric.

Derivation

  1. Slow motion gives \(\tau \approx t\), and leaves only the \(00\) term: \(\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu{}_{00} = 0\).
  2. For a static metric: \(\Gamma^\mu{}_{00} = -\tfrac12g^{\mu i}\partial_ig_{00}\).
  3. In a weak field, \(g^{\mu\nu} \approx \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu}\), so \(\Gamma^0{}_{00} \approx 0\) and \(\Gamma^i{}_{00} \approx \tfrac12\partial_ih_{00}\).
  4. So \(\ddot x^i = -\tfrac12\partial_ih_{00}\). Comparing with \(\ddot x^i = -\partial_i\phi\) gives \(h_{00} \approx 2\phi\).

For a static metric, \(p_0\) is conserved. In the Newtonian limit, \(p_0 = m + m\phi + \vec p^{\,2}/2m + \dots\), which is rest energy, gravitational energy and kinetic energy.

"Matter tells space-time how to curve." The field equations are fixed almost uniquely by three requirements.

Einstein field equations
\[ G_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu} \]

\(G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}R\), with \(c = 1\). Ten coupled non-linear PDEs for the metric. \(\Lambda\) is the cosmological constant.

Motivation

  1. The Newtonian limit requires \(\tilde G_{00} \approx \Delta g_{00}\) with \(\tilde G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}\), for some symmetric tensor \(\tilde G_{\mu\nu}\).
  2. Since \(\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0\), it must be covariantly conserved.
  3. It must be linear in second derivatives of the metric, with none higher.
  4. Only the Einstein tensor and the metric itself satisfy these, so \(\tilde G_{\mu\nu} = \alpha G_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}\). The weak-field limit fixes \(\alpha = 1\).

Trace-reversed form

Contracting with \(g^{\mu\nu}\), in four dimensions, with \(T = g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}\) and \(\kappa = 8\pi G\), gives \(R = -4\Lambda - \kappa T\). Substituting back:

\[ R_{\mu\nu} = -\Lambda g_{\mu\nu} + \kappa\big(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12Tg_{\mu\nu}\big). \]
Vacuum Einstein equations
\[ R_{\mu\nu} = -\Lambda g_{\mu\nu} \]

With \(\Lambda = 0\), this is \(R_{\mu\nu} = 0\). For \(\Lambda \neq 0\), space-time cannot be flat.

The cosmological constant. Einstein added \(\Lambda\) in 1917 for a static universe, then regretted it after Hubble's discovery. A small positive value, \(\Lambda \approx 10^{-52}\,\text{m}^{-2}\), may now explain dark energy.

The first exact solution, and the basis of every solar-system test: the field outside a static, spherical mass.

Schwarzschild metric
\[ ds^2 = \left(1 - \frac{r_S}{r}\right)dt^2 - \left(1 - \frac{r_S}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 - r^2\big(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\big) \] \[ r_S = \frac{2G_NM}{c^2} \]

The Sun has \(r_S \approx 3\,\text{km}\), and Earth has \(r_S \approx 9\,\text{mm}\). The solution holds only outside the body.

Derivation

  1. A static, spherically symmetric metric can be brought to the form \[ ds^2 = e^{2f(r)}dt^2 - e^{2g(r)}dr^2 - r^2\big(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\big). \]
  2. Computing the Ricci tensor: \[ R_{00} = (f'' - g'f' + f'^2 + 2f'r^{-1})e^{2f-2g}, \quad R_{11} = -f'' + g'f' - f'^2 + 2g'r^{-1}, \quad R_{22} = 1 - (1 + rf' - rg')e^{-2g}. \]
  3. \(R_{00}e^{-2f+2g} + R_{11} = 0\) gives \(f' + g' = 0\). A rescaling of \(t\) then allows \(f = -g\).
  4. \(R_{22} = 0\) becomes \((re^{2f})' = 1\), so \(re^{2f} = r + C\).
  5. The Newtonian limit \(e^{2f} \approx 1 + 2\phi = 1 - 2G_NM/r\) fixes \(C = -2G_NM\).

Geometry

  • Birkhoff's theorem: any spherically symmetric vacuum solution is static, so Schwarzschild is the only one.
  • \(r\) is not radial distance. Radial distance is \(d = \int dr\,(1 - r_S/r)^{-1/2} \gt r_2 - r_1\).
  • \(r\) is defined by area. Spheres of constant \(t, r\) have area \(4\pi r^2\), so space is non-Euclidean.
  • Gravitational time dilation. A static clock has \(d\tau = (1 - r_S/r)^{1/2}dt\), so the lower clock runs slower.
  • \(r = r_S\) is a coordinate singularity, not a physical one: curvature stays finite there.

There is one unstable circular orbit, at \(r = \tfrac32r_S\).

The two classical tests of Einstein's theory, using \(u = 1/r\). Both come from a small correction to a Newtonian equation.

Perihelion shift
\begin{equation} \Delta\phi \approx \frac{6\pi\tilde M^2}{L^2} \approx \frac{6\pi\tilde M}{r_{\min}(1 + \epsilon)}, \qquad \tilde M = \frac{r_S}{2} \tag{171} \end{equation}

Per orbit. For Mercury this is \(5\times10^{-7}\) rad, or 43″ per century.

Perihelion: outline

  1. With \(u = 1/r\) as a function of \(\phi\), the orbit equation is \[ u'' + u = \frac{\tilde M}{L^2} + 3\tilde Mu^2. \] The last term is the relativistic correction.
  2. Writing \(y = u - \tilde M/L^2\) and dropping \(y^2\) terms gives a forced oscillator with \(k^2 = 1 - 6\tilde M^2/L^2\).
  3. So \(u \approx \dfrac{\tilde M}{L^2}\big(1 + \epsilon\cos(k\phi - \phi_0)\big)\), up to a small correction.
  4. Successive perihelia are separated by \(\delta\phi = 2\pi/k \approx 2\pi(1 + 3\tilde M^2/L^2)\), which is more than \(2\pi\). So the orbit does not close.
Deflection of light
\[ \Delta\phi \approx \frac{2r_S}{b} = \frac{4G_NM}{c^2b} \]

For light grazing the Sun, \(b = r_{\text{sun}}\), this gives \(8.48\times10^{-6}\) rad, or 1.75″. Confirmed by Eddington in 1919.

Deflection: outline

  1. Using \(d\phi/d\lambda = L/r^2\) and \(u = 1/r\), the null orbit satisfies \begin{equation} u'^2 = \frac{E^2}{L^2} - u^2 + r_Su^3 \tag{172} \end{equation} and, on differentiating, \begin{equation} u'' + u = \tfrac32r_Su^2. \tag{173} \end{equation}
  2. Neglecting the right side gives \(u = \tfrac1b\sin\phi\): a straight line with impact parameter \(b\).
  3. Set \(u = \tfrac1b\big(\sin\phi + \tfrac{r_S}{2b}v(\phi)\big)\). To first order, \(v'' + v = 3\sin^2\phi\), solved by \(v = (1 - \cos\phi)^2\).
  4. So \(u = \tfrac1b\sin\phi + \tfrac{r_S}{2b^2}(1 - \cos\phi)^2\).
  5. Set \(u = 0\) at \(\phi = \pi + \Delta\phi\), using \(\sin\phi \approx -\Delta\phi\) and \(\cos\phi \approx -1\). This gives \(\Delta\phi \approx 2r_S/b\).

A falling observer crosses the horizon in finite proper time. To a distant observer, they never quite arrive.

Radial fall from rest at \(r_0\)
\[ \dot r^2 = r_S\left(\frac1r - \frac{1}{r_0}\right) \]

The equation of motion with \(L = 0\) and \(E^2 = V^2(r_0)\). It has the same form as in Newton's theory.

ObserverTime to reach \(r_S\)
Falling particle (proper time \(\tau\))Finite: \(\tau = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{r_S}}\displaystyle\int_{r_S}^{r_0}\left(\frac{r_0r}{r_0 - r}\right)^{1/2}dr\)
Distant static observer (coordinate time \(t\))Infinite: \(t \sim -\ln\epsilon\) to reach \(r_S + \epsilon\)

The coordinate speed for a fall from far away is \(\tilde v = (r_S/r)^{1/2}(1 - r_S/r)\), which tends to 0 at \(r_S\). The Newtonian speed would instead be \((r_S/r)^{1/2}\).

Gravitational redshift
\[ \frac{\nu_2}{\nu_1} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - r_S/r_1}{1 - r_S/r_2}} \]

For static observers, with \(r_1 \lt r_2\). An outward signal is redshifted, and \(\nu_2 \to 0\) as \(r_1 \to r_S\).

The event horizon

\(r = r_S\) is the boundary of a region invisible from outside. It can only be crossed in one direction. Only extremely dense objects are smaller than their Schwarzschild radius, and these are black holes.

Inside the horizon

The coordinate singularity is removed by advanced Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates, \(v = t + r + r_S\ln(r/r_S - 1)\), in which

\[ ds^2 = \left(1 - \frac{r_S}{r}\right)dv^2 - 2dv\,dr - r^2\big(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\big), \]

which is regular for all \(r \gt 0\), and continues the metric to \(r \lt r_S\).

Calculate the Schwarzschild radius of the Sun. What density would the Sun's mass have, if compressed to this radius?

Show solution
  1. \(r_S = \dfrac{2(6.67\times10^{-11})(2\times10^{30})}{9\times10^{16}} \approx 2.96\,\text{km}\).
  2. \(\rho = \dfrac{M}{\frac43\pi r_S^3} \approx \dfrac{2\times10^{30}}{1.09\times10^{11}} \approx 1.8\times10^{19}\,\text{kg m}^{-3}\).
  3. For comparison, nuclear density is around \(2\times10^{17}\,\text{kg m}^{-3}\).

Show that circular orbits exist only for \(L^2 \geq 3r_S^2\). Find the innermost stable circular orbit, and its value of \(E^2\).

Show solution
  1. Expanding, \(V^2 = 1 - \dfrac{r_S}{r} + \dfrac{L^2}{r^2} - \dfrac{r_SL^2}{r^3}\).
  2. \(\dfrac{dV^2}{dr} = \dfrac{r_S}{r^2} - \dfrac{2L^2}{r^3} + \dfrac{3r_SL^2}{r^4} = 0\) gives \(r_Sr^2 - 2L^2r + 3r_SL^2 = 0\).
  3. So \(r_\pm = \dfrac{L^2}{r_S}\left(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{3r_S^2}{L^2}}\right)\). These are real only if \(L^2 \geq 3r_S^2\).
  4. The stable orbit moves inward as \(L\) falls. At \(L^2 = 3r_S^2\) the two roots merge at \(r = 3r_S\).
  5. There, \(E^2 = V^2 = \big(1 - \tfrac13\big)\big(1 + \tfrac13\big) = \tfrac89\).

Show that the photon potential \(V^2 = (1 - r_S/r)L^2/r^2\) has a maximum at \(r = \tfrac32r_S\). Why is this orbit unstable? What is \(V^2\) there, in terms of \(L\) and \(r_S\)?

Show solution
  1. \(V^2 = L^2\big(r^{-2} - r_Sr^{-3}\big)\), so \(\dfrac{dV^2}{dr} = L^2\big(-2r^{-3} + 3r_Sr^{-4}\big)\).
  2. Setting this to zero gives \(r = \tfrac32r_S\).
  3. \(V^2 \to 0\) as \(r \to r_S\) and as \(r \to \infty\), and it is positive between. So this critical point is a maximum, and a small push sends the photon inward or outward.
  4. \(V^2 = \big(1 - \tfrac23\big)\dfrac{4L^2}{9r_S^2} = \dfrac{4L^2}{27r_S^2}\).

Mercury has \(r_{\min}(1 + \epsilon) \approx 5.55\times10^{10}\,\text{m}\) and an orbital period of 88 days. Taking \(\tilde M = 1.48\,\text{km}\) for the Sun, find its perihelion shift per orbit, and per century, in arc seconds.

Show solution
  1. From (171): \[ \Delta\phi \approx \frac{6\pi(1480)}{5.55\times10^{10}} \approx 5.03\times10^{-7}\,\text{rad per orbit}. \]
  2. In a century, the number of orbits is \(36525/88 \approx 415\).
  3. So the total is \(2.09\times10^{-4}\) rad. With \(1\,\text{rad} = 206265''\), this is about \(43''\) per century.

Check by substitution that \(v = (1 - \cos\phi)^2\) solves \(v'' + v = 3\sin^2\phi\). Find the deflection of light grazing Jupiter, with \(M = 1.9\times10^{27}\,\text{kg}\) and radius \(7\times10^7\,\text{m}\).

Show solution
  1. \(v = 1 - 2\cos\phi + \cos^2\phi\), so \(v' = 2\sin\phi - 2\sin\phi\cos\phi\).
  2. \(v'' = 2\cos\phi - 2\cos^2\phi + 2\sin^2\phi\).
  3. \(v'' + v = 1 - \cos^2\phi + 2\sin^2\phi = 3\sin^2\phi\). ✓
  4. For Jupiter, \(r_S = 2G_NM/c^2 \approx 2.8\,\text{m}\). So \(\Delta\phi \approx \dfrac{2(2.8)}{7\times10^7} \approx 8\times10^{-8}\) rad, or about \(0.017''\).

A particle falls from rest at infinity, so \(E^2 = 1\), and \(\dot r^2 = r_S/r\). Find the proper time to go from \(r_1\) to \(r_2 \lt r_1\). What is the proper time from \(r_S\) to \(r = 0\)?

Show solution
  1. Taking the inward root, \(\dfrac{d\tau}{dr} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{r}{r_S}}\).
  2. Integrating: \[ \tau = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_S}}\int_{r_2}^{r_1}\sqrt r\,dr = \frac{2}{3\sqrt{r_S}}\big(r_1^{3/2} - r_2^{3/2}\big). \]
  3. This is finite at \(r_S\): nothing special happens to the particle at the horizon.
  4. From \(r_S\) to 0, \(\tau = \tfrac23r_S\), which is \(\tfrac23r_S/c\) with \(c\) restored.

Light is emitted by a static source at \(r = \tfrac32r_S\), and received at infinity. Find the ratio of frequencies. By how much does a clock at this radius run slow?

Show solution
  1. With \(r_2 \to \infty\): \[ \frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_1} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{r_S}{r_1}} = \sqrt{\frac13} \approx 0.577. \]
  2. The clock has \(d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - r_S/r}\), the same factor.
  3. So the clock runs at 57.7% of the rate at infinity.

Find the proper radial distance from \(r = r_S\) to \(r = 2r_S\), at constant \(t\). Compare with the difference \(r_S\) in the coordinate. You may use \[ \int\frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - r_S/r}} = \sqrt{r(r - r_S)} + \frac{r_S}{2}\ln\big(2r - r_S + 2\sqrt{r(r - r_S)}\big). \]

Show solution
  1. \(d = \displaystyle\int_{r_S}^{2r_S}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - r_S/r}}\), which is finite despite the singular metric component.
  2. At \(r = 2r_S\): \(\sqrt2\,r_S + \tfrac{r_S}{2}\ln\big((3 + 2\sqrt2)r_S\big)\).
  3. At \(r = r_S\): \(\tfrac{r_S}{2}\ln r_S\).
  4. Subtracting: \(d = r_S\big[\sqrt2 + \tfrac12\ln(3 + 2\sqrt2)\big] \approx 2.30r_S\). That is larger than the difference \(r_S\) in the coordinate.

A satellite clock orbits at \(r = 2.66\times10^7\,\text{m}\). Ignoring its orbital speed, how much faster does it run than a clock on Earth's surface, \(r = 6.37\times10^6\,\text{m}\)? Take \(r_S = 8.87\,\text{mm}\) for Earth. Express this in microseconds per day.

Show solution
  1. \(d\tau/dt = \sqrt{1 - r_S/r} \approx 1 - r_S/2r\).
  2. The fractional difference is \[ \frac{r_S}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_E} - \frac{1}{r}\right) = 4.435\times10^{-3}\big(1.570 - 0.376\big)\times10^{-7} \approx 5.3\times10^{-10}. \]
  3. Over a day of \(86400\,\text{s}\), this is about \(46\,\mu\text{s}\).

Derive the trace-reversed form of the Einstein equations. Show that in vacuum, \(R = -4\Lambda\).

Show solution
  1. Contract \(R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}R - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \kappa T_{\mu\nu}\) with \(g^{\mu\nu}\), using \(g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = 4\).
  2. This gives \(R - 2R - 4\Lambda = \kappa T\), so \(R = -4\Lambda - \kappa T\).
  3. Substituting back: \[ R_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}(-4\Lambda - \kappa T) + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} + \kappa T_{\mu\nu} = -\Lambda g_{\mu\nu} + \kappa\big(T_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12Tg_{\mu\nu}\big). \]
  4. In vacuum, \(T_{\mu\nu} = 0\), so \(T = 0\) and \(R = -4\Lambda\).

For the perfect fluid \(T^{\mu\nu} = (\epsilon + p)u^\mu u^\nu - pg^{\mu\nu}\), check that the rest frame gives \(\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon, p, p, p)\) in Minkowski space. Show that the trace is \(\epsilon - 3p\), and that it vanishes for radiation.

Show solution
  1. In the rest frame, \(u^\mu = (1, \vec 0)\) and \(g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu}\).
  2. \(T^{00} = \epsilon + p - p = \epsilon\). \(T^{0i} = 0\). \(T^{ij} = -p\eta^{ij} = p\delta^{ij}\). ✓
  3. \(T = g_{\mu\nu}T^{\mu\nu} = (\epsilon + p)u^\mu u_\mu - 4p = \epsilon + p - 4p = \epsilon - 3p\), using \(u^\mu u_\mu = 1\).
  4. For radiation, \(p = \tfrac13\epsilon\), so \(T = 0\).

Using \(g_{00} \approx 1 + 2\phi/c^2\) with \(\phi = -G_NM/r\), estimate \(|h_{00}|\) at Earth's surface and at the Sun's surface. Earth: \(r_S = 8.87\,\text{mm}\), \(r = 6.37\times10^6\,\text{m}\). Sun: \(r_S = 2.95\,\text{km}\), \(r = 6.96\times10^8\,\text{m}\). Is the weak-field assumption good?

Show solution
  1. \(h_{00} = 2\phi/c^2 = -2G_NM/(rc^2) = -r_S/r\).
  2. Earth: \(|h_{00}| = \dfrac{8.87\times10^{-3}}{6.37\times10^6} \approx 1.4\times10^{-9}\).
  3. Sun: \(|h_{00}| = \dfrac{2950}{6.96\times10^8} \approx 4.2\times10^{-6}\).
  4. Both are far smaller than 1, so the weak-field assumption is very good in the solar system. It fails only near \(r \sim r_S\).
