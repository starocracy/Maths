Light behaves as a wave and as a particle, and so does matter. The same two relations connect both pictures.

Planck and de Broglie
\[ E = h\nu, \qquad p = \frac h\lambda \]

With \(\hbar = h/2\pi\), \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) and \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\), these become \(E = \hbar\omega\) and \(p = \hbar k\).

Light as a wave

Light diffracts through a slit of about one wavelength, and interferes when it can take two routes. Waves added in phase interfere constructively, and waves in anti-phase cancel. A double slit gives light and dark stripes on a screen.

Unlike water waves, light comes in discrete quanta. Even when dimmed so that photons arrive one at a time, an interference pattern still builds up.

Light as a particle: the photoelectric effect

Light on a metal surface knocks out electrons. There is a threshold frequency \(\nu_0\), depending on the metal, below which no electrons are released. Above it, the electron energy rises linearly with \(\nu\). The intensity only changes the number of electrons, not their energy.

Einstein's photoelectric equation
\begin{equation} E_{\text{electron}} = E_{\text{photon}} - E_{\text{escape}} = h\nu - E_{\text{escape}} \tag{1.2} \end{equation}

One photon gives all of its energy to one electron.

Matter as a wave

Electrons, neutrons, atoms and even \(C_{60}\) molecules show diffraction and interference. The double-slit pattern is not the sum of two single-slit patterns. Waves are in step when

\begin{equation} l_1 - l_2 = n\lambda, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \tag{1.3} \end{equation}

Where \(p = h/\lambda\) comes from

For light, \(\nu\lambda = c\) and \(p = E/c\). Combining with \(E = h\nu\):

\[ p = \frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac h\lambda. \]

The same relation holds for electrons and other matter. Quantum effects are generally harder to see the larger the object.

Quantisation is not mysterious mathematically: it is what boundary conditions do to the solutions of a differential equation.

Quantisation from boundary conditions
\[ f'' + \alpha^2f = 0, \quad f(0) = f(L) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \frac{n\pi}{L} \]

Only discrete values of \(\alpha\) allow a non-trivial solution. \(n\) is the quantum number.

The calculation

  1. Trying \(f = Ae^{\lambda x}\) gives \(\lambda^2 + \alpha^2 = 0\), so \(\lambda = \pm i\alpha\). The general solution is \begin{equation} f(x) = A\sin(\alpha x) + B\cos(\alpha x). \tag{1.12} \end{equation}
  2. \(f(0) = 0\) gives \(B = 0\).
  3. \(f(L) = 0\) gives \(A\sin(\alpha L) = 0\). For a non-trivial solution, \(\alpha L = n\pi\).
  4. So there are infinitely many solutions, and the general solution is their superposition: \begin{equation} \psi_n(x) = A_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}. \tag{1.16} \end{equation}

The \(\psi_n\) are the eigenfunctions of the ODE. In QM they are the eigenstates, and any quantum state is a superposition of them.

The rules of the game. These cannot be derived from classical mechanics: they are the starting point.

Born rule
\[ P(x) = \psi^*(x)\psi(x), \qquad P(a \leq x \leq b) = \int_a^b\psi^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx \]

For normalised \(\psi\). The wave function gives a probability distribution, not an exact position.

The postulates

  1. State. A system is represented by a complex wave function \(\psi(x,t)\). It is a mathematical construct, not a physical object like the height of a wave.
  2. Superposition. Wave functions form a complex vector space: if \(\psi_1, \psi_2\) are wave functions, so is \(\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2\).
  3. Scalar product. In Dirac notation, with bra \(\langle\psi_1|\) and ket \(|\psi_2\rangle\): \begin{equation} \langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\psi_1^*(x)\psi_2(x)\,dx. \tag{2.2} \end{equation}
Order matters. \(\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\) and \(\langle\psi_2|\psi_1\rangle\) are complex conjugates of each other, not equal. The bra is the one that gets complex conjugated.

Normalisation

\(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \|\psi\|^2\) is real and positive. The total probability must be one, so \(|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\) is the normalised wave function. If \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\) is not a finite positive number, the wave function cannot be normalised and has no probability interpretation.

Measurement and collapse

Making a measurement changes the system. After a position measurement gives \(x_0\), the new \(\psi\) is concentrated at \(x_0\), and the next measurement gives a nearly identical answer. This is the collapse of the wave function. To find a distribution, you need many identical systems, not many measurements of one.

Since single outcomes are probabilistic, the useful predictions are averages. Every observable has an operator.

Expectation value
\begin{equation} \langle A\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat A\psi\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\psi^*(x)\,\hat A\psi(x)\,dx \tag{2.17} \end{equation}

For normalised states. The operator acts first, then you integrate.

Position and momentum operators
\[ \hat x = x, \qquad \hat p_x = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \]

For any function of position, \(\langle f\rangle = \int\psi^*f(x)\psi\,dx\).

The momentum operator is justified by the plane wave \(\psi = e^{2\pi ix/\lambda}\), which gives \(\hat p_x\psi = (h/\lambda)\psi = p\psi\). This is an eigenvalue problem, with the matrix replaced by a differential operator. The plane wave itself cannot be normalised.

Example\(\psi = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(3\pi x/L)\) on \(0 \lt x \lt L\)

Acting with the momentum operator,

\[ \hat p_x\psi = -i\hbar\sqrt{\frac2L}\frac{3\pi}{L}\cos\frac{3\pi x}{L}, \]

so \(\psi\) is not an eigenfunction of \(\hat p_x\). The average momentum is

\[ \langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\frac{3\pi}{L^2}\int_0^L\sin\frac{6\pi x}{L}\,dx = 0. \]

Zero average momentum does not imply that the particle is at rest.

Measured values are real numbers, so observables must be Hermitian operators.

Hermitian operator
\begin{equation} \langle\psi_1|\hat A\psi_2\rangle = \langle\hat A\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle \quad\text{for all } \psi_1, \psi_2 \tag{2.21} \end{equation}

For matrices this means \(H^\dagger = (H^*)^T = H\).

A useful consequence, for any Hermitian \(\hat A\):

\begin{equation} \langle\psi_1|\hat A\psi_2\rangle = \langle\psi_2|\hat A\psi_1\rangle^*. \tag{2.27} \end{equation}

Setting \(\psi_1 = \psi_2 = \psi\) shows \(\langle\psi|\hat A\psi\rangle\) equals its own conjugate. So the expectation value of a Hermitian operator is real.

ExampleMatrix test
\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1+i \\ 1-i & 3 \end{pmatrix} \]

is Hermitian, since transposing and conjugating gives \(A\) back. The matrix with \(1+i\) in both off-diagonal positions is not.

Example\(\hat p\) is Hermitian

Integrate by parts:

\[ \langle\psi_1|\hat P\psi_2\rangle = -i\hbar\int\psi_1^*\frac{d\psi_2}{dx}\,dx = -i\hbar\big[\psi_1^*\psi_2\big]_{-\infty}^\infty + i\hbar\int\frac{d\psi_1^*}{dx}\psi_2\,dx. \]

For normalisable wave functions, \(\psi_{1,2} \to 0\) at infinity, so the surface term vanishes. What remains is

\[ \int\left(-i\hbar\frac{d\psi_1}{dx}\right)^*\psi_2\,dx = \langle\hat P\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle. \]

The factor of \(i\) is essential: \(d/dx\) on its own is not Hermitian.

ExampleA three-state system
\[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

\(\langle\psi| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, -i, 1)\), with the bra conjugated, so \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1\). Then

\[ \langle\psi|\hat A\psi\rangle = \tfrac13(1, -i, 1)\begin{pmatrix} 3 + i \\ 4 \\ 2 + 3i \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac13(3 + i - 4i + 2 + 3i) = \frac53. \]

The imaginary parts cancel, as they must.

The eigenfunctions of an observable are a basis, and the only values a measurement can ever give are its eigenvalues.

Eigenvalue equation
\begin{equation} \hat A\psi_n(x) = \lambda_n\psi_n(x) \tag{2.33} \end{equation}

For Hermitian \(\hat A\): the eigenvalues are real, and the eigenfunctions can be chosen orthonormal, \(\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}\).

Expansion and measurement probabilities
\[ |\psi\rangle = \sum_na_n|\psi_n\rangle, \qquad a_i = \langle\psi_i|\psi\rangle, \qquad P(\lambda_i) = |a_i|^2 \]

After measuring \(\lambda_i\), the state is \(\psi_i\), and repeating the measurement gives \(\lambda_i\) with probability one.

Proofs

Eigenvalues are real

From Hermiticity, \(\langle\hat A\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle = \langle\psi_n|\hat A\psi_n\rangle\). The left side is \(\lambda_n^*\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle\), and the right side is \(\lambda_n\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle\). Since \(\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle \neq 0\), \(\lambda_n = \lambda_n^*\).

Different eigenvalues give orthogonal eigenfunctions

\(\langle\psi_n|\hat A\psi_m\rangle = \langle\hat A\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle\) gives \((\lambda_m - \lambda_n)\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle = 0\). So either \(\lambda_m = \lambda_n\) or \(\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle = 0\). Degenerate eigenfunctions, with equal eigenvalues, can be chosen orthogonal.

Probabilities sum to one

\(1 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m,n}a_m^*a_n\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \sum_n|a_n|^2\). So the eigenvalues exhaust the possible results: in QM, no other values can be obtained.

Measuring something else. To measure a different observable \(B\), rewrite the state in the eigenbasis of \(\hat B\).

The Hamiltonian

Replacing classical observables by operators in \(E = p^2/2m + V\) gives the most important operator in QM. Its eigenvalues are the energy levels:

Time-independent Schrödinger equation
\begin{equation} \hat H\psi = E\psi, \qquad \hat H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V(\hat x) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + V(x) \tag{2.54} \end{equation}

In 3D, \(\dfrac{\hat{\mathbf p}^2}{2m} = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial z^2}\right)\).

ExampleParticle on a ring: periodic boundary conditions

With \(\psi(0) = \psi(L)\) and no force, \(\hat p\psi = p\psi\) gives \(\psi = Ae^{ikx}\), with \(k = p/\hbar\). The boundary condition requires \(e^{ikL} = 1\), so

\[ k = \frac{2n\pi}{L}, \qquad \psi_n(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}e^{2in\pi x/L}, \]

with \(A_n = 1/\sqrt L\) from normalisation. For \(m \neq n\), \(\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle = \tfrac1L\dfrac{L(e^{2\pi i(m-n)} - 1)}{2\pi i(m-n)} = 0\).

The Schrödinger equation is the law of motion for the wave function. Energy eigenstates just rotate in phase, while superpositions truly change.

Time-dependent Schrödinger equation
\begin{equation} i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(x,t) = \hat H\psi(x,t) \tag{2.56} \end{equation}
General solution
\begin{equation} \psi(x,t) = \sum_nc_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\psi_n(x), \qquad E_n = \hbar\omega_n \tag{2.63} \end{equation}

The \(c_n\) are constant, and \(|c_n|^2\) stays the probability of measuring \(E_n\). Also \(\langle\hat H\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2E_n\).

Stationary states

Separating variables, \(\psi = f(t)\psi_n(x)\), gives \(i\hbar\,df/dt = E_nf\), so

\begin{equation} \psi(x,t) = e^{-i(t - t_0)E_n/\hbar}\psi_n(x). \tag{2.61} \end{equation}

For a single eigenstate, the phases cancel in \(\psi^*\psi\), so the probability distribution is time-independent.

Superpositions

For \(\psi = a\phi_ne^{-i\omega_nt} + b\phi_me^{-i\omega_mt}\):

\begin{equation} P = |a|^2|\phi_n|^2 + |b|^2|\phi_m|^2 + b^*a\phi_m^*\phi_ne^{i(\omega_m - \omega_n)t} + a^*b\phi_n^*\phi_me^{i(\omega_n - \omega_m)t}. \tag{2.82} \end{equation}

The cross terms oscillate with frequency \(|\omega_n - \omega_m|\). Integrated over all \(x\), they vanish by orthogonality, so the total probability stays constant while the shape moves.

Conservation of probability

Using \(\partial_t\psi = \hat H\psi/(i\hbar)\) and Hermiticity:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \frac i\hbar\big(\langle\hat H\psi|\psi\rangle - \langle\psi|\hat H\psi\rangle\big) = 0. \]
ExampleTwo-state system: \(\hat H = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1+i \\ 1-i & 2 \end{pmatrix}\)

Energies. \(\det(\hat H - E) = (E - \hbar)(E - 2\hbar) - 2\hbar^2 = E(E - 3\hbar) = 0\), so \(E = 0\) or \(3\hbar\).

Eigenstates. Solving and normalising,

\[ |\psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix} 1+i \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\psi_1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1-i \end{pmatrix}. \]

These are orthogonal, as they must be: \(\langle\psi_1|\psi_0\rangle = \tfrac13(1 + i - (1 + i)) = 0\).

Time evolution. If \(|\psi(0)\rangle = a|\psi_0\rangle + b|\psi_1\rangle\), then

\[ |\psi(t)\rangle = a|\psi_0\rangle + be^{-3it}|\psi_1\rangle. \]
Correspondence principle. Superpositions of many eigenstates produce sharper peaks that move back and forth, like a classical particle bouncing off walls.

Find the eigenvalues and normalised eigenvectors of \(M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). What property of \(M\) guarantees that the eigenvalues are real? Check that the eigenvectors are orthogonal.

Show solution
  1. \(\det(M - \lambda) = (1 - \lambda)^2 - 4 = 0\), so \(\lambda - 1 = \pm2\), giving \(\lambda = 3\) or \(-1\).
  2. For \(\lambda = 3\): \(-2a + 2ib = 0\), so \(a = ib\). Normalised, \(\mathbf e_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(i, 1)^T\).
  3. For \(\lambda = -1\): \(2a + 2ib = 0\), so \(a = -ib\). Normalised, \(\mathbf e_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-i, 1)^T\).
  4. \(M\) is Hermitian: \((M^*)^T = M\). That guarantees real eigenvalues.
  5. Orthogonality, conjugating the bra: \(\mathbf e_1^\dagger\mathbf e_2 = \tfrac12\big[(-i)(-i) + 1\big] = \tfrac12(-1 + 1) = 0\). ✓

The unnormalised wave function of a particle is \(\psi(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}\).

(i) Find the normalised wave function. (ii) Find the probability that a position measurement gives \(x \gt 1\). (iii) Find \(\langle p\rangle\). (iv) Find \(\langle x^2\rangle\).

Hint: use \(x = \tan\theta\).

Show solution
  1. (i) With \(x = \tan\theta\), \(dx = \sec^2\theta\,d\theta\): \[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{(1 + x^2)^2} = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \frac\pi2. \] So \(\hat\psi = \sqrt{\dfrac2\pi}\,\dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}\).
  2. (ii) \(x = 1\) corresponds to \(\theta = \pi/4\): \[ P = \frac2\pi\int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \frac2\pi\left[\frac\theta2 + \frac{\sin2\theta}{4}\right]_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2} = \frac2\pi\left(\frac\pi8 - \frac14\right) = \frac14 - \frac{1}{2\pi} \approx 0.091. \]
  3. (iii) \(\psi\) is real and even, so \(\psi\psi'\) is odd and \[ \langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\int\hat\psi\hat\psi'\,dx = 0. \]
  4. (iv) Again with \(x = \tan\theta\): \[ \langle x^2\rangle = \frac2\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \frac2\pi\cdot\frac\pi2 = 1. \]

A quantum system is in the normalised state \(|\psi\rangle = n\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \\ 1 + i \end{pmatrix}\). Find \(n\), and the expectation value of the observable \[ Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & i & 0 \\ -i & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Show solution
  1. \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = |n|^2(1 + 1 + 2) = 4|n|^2 = 1\), so \(n = \tfrac12\).
  2. Acting with \(Q\) on the unscaled vector: \[ Q\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \\ 1 + i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 1 \\ -i - 2i \\ 1 + i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -3i \\ 1 + i \end{pmatrix}. \]
  3. The bra is \(\tfrac12(1, i, 1 - i)\). So \[ \langle Q\rangle = \tfrac14\big[3 + (i)(-3i) + (1 - i)(1 + i)\big] = \tfrac14(3 + 3 + 2) = 2. \] This is real, as expected for a Hermitian matrix.

Establish whether each of the following is Hermitian, on wave functions that vanish with their derivatives at \(x \to \pm\infty\):

(a) \(\hat D = \dfrac{d}{dx}\)   (b) \(\hat F = i\dfrac{d}{dx}\)   (c) \(\hat G = \dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}\)

Show solution
  1. (a) Integrating by parts, with zero surface term: \[ \langle\psi_1|\hat D\psi_2\rangle = \int\psi_1^*\psi_2'\,dx = -\int(\psi_1^*)'\psi_2\,dx = -\langle\hat D\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle. \] So \(\hat D\) is not Hermitian. It is anti-Hermitian.
  2. (b) \(\langle\psi_1|\hat F\psi_2\rangle = i\int\psi_1^*\psi_2'\,dx = -i\int(\psi_1')^*\psi_2\,dx = \int(i\psi_1')^*\psi_2\,dx = \langle\hat F\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle\). So \(\hat F\) is Hermitian.
  3. (c) Integrating by parts twice brings two minus signs: \[ \int\psi_1^*\psi_2''\,dx = \int(\psi_1'')^*\psi_2\,dx. \] So \(\hat G\) is Hermitian.

A two-state system has Hamiltonian \(\hat H = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), and at \(t = 0\) it is in the state \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\). Find the energy eigenvalues and eigenstates, and the state at time \(t\). Find the probability that the system is still in \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\) at time \(t\).

Show solution
  1. \((2\hbar - E)^2 = \hbar^2\), so \(E = \hbar\) or \(3\hbar\). The normalised eigenstates are \[ |\psi_-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\psi_+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \] with energies \(\hbar\) and \(3\hbar\) respectively.
  2. The initial state is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\psi_+\rangle + |\psi_-\rangle\big)\).
  3. Each part picks up its own phase: \[ |\psi(t)\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(e^{-3it}|\psi_+\rangle + e^{-it}|\psi_-\rangle\big) = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} e^{-3it} + e^{-it} \\ e^{-3it} - e^{-it} \end{pmatrix}. \]
  4. The overlap with \((1,0)^T\) is the top component, \(e^{-2it}\cos t\). So \[ P(t) = \cos^2t. \] The system oscillates between the two basis states.

Find the eigenvalues and normalised eigenvectors \(\mathbf p, \mathbf q, \mathbf r\) of \(M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Write \((1, 0, 0)^T\) as \(a\mathbf p + b\mathbf q + c\mathbf r\), and check that \(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 1\).

Show solution
  1. With \(\mu = \lambda - 2\), the characteristic equation is \(-\mu^3 + 2\mu = 0\), so \(\mu = 0, \pm\sqrt2\). Thus \(\lambda = 2\) and \(2 \pm \sqrt2\).
  2. The normalised eigenvectors are \[ \mathbf p = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf q = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \sqrt2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf r = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -\sqrt2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \] for \(\lambda = 2\), \(2 + \sqrt2\) and \(2 - \sqrt2\).
  3. By orthonormality, each coefficient is a scalar product with \((1,0,0)^T\): \(a = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\), \(b = \tfrac12\), \(c = \tfrac12\).
  4. Check: \(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1\). ✓

Show that \([\sigma_3, \sigma_+] = 2\sigma_+\). Is \(\sigma_+\) Hermitian?

Show solution
  1. \(\sigma_+ = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\).
  2. \(\sigma_3\sigma_+ = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\sigma_+\sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\).
  3. So \([\sigma_3, \sigma_+] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 2\sigma_+\). ✓

Prove that the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real, and that eigenfunctions with different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Show solution
  1. Let \(\hat A\psi_n = \lambda_n\psi_n\). Hermiticity gives \(\langle\hat A\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle = \langle\psi_n|\hat A\psi_n\rangle\).
  2. The bra is conjugated, so the left side is \(\lambda_n^*\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle\). The right side is \(\lambda_n\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle\).
  3. Since \(\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle \neq 0\), \(\lambda_n^* = \lambda_n\), so \(\lambda_n\) is real.
  4. Now start from \(\langle\psi_n|\hat A\psi_m\rangle = \langle\hat A\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle\). Using reality, this gives \(\lambda_m\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle = \lambda_n\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle\).
  5. So \((\lambda_m - \lambda_n)\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle = 0\). If \(\lambda_m \neq \lambda_n\), then \(\langle\psi_n|\psi_m\rangle = 0\).

Use the Schrödinger equation and the Hermiticity of \(\hat H\) to prove that \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 0\). What does this mean physically?

Show solution
  1. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \displaystyle\int\left(\frac{\partial\psi^*}{\partial t}\psi + \psi^*\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\right)dx\).
  2. From the Schrödinger equation, \(\partial_t\psi = \dfrac{1}{i\hbar}\hat H\psi\). Conjugating, \(\partial_t\psi^* = -\dfrac{1}{i\hbar}(\hat H\psi)^*\).
  3. Substituting: \[ \frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\big(\langle\psi|\hat H\psi\rangle - \langle\hat H\psi|\psi\rangle\big) = 0, \] by Hermiticity.
  4. Physically, total probability is conserved. Particles are not being created or destroyed.

A particle moves on a ring of circumference \(L\), so that \(\psi(x + L) = \psi(x)\), with \(V = 0\). Find the momentum eigenfunctions, the allowed momenta and the energies. Which levels are degenerate?

Show solution
  1. \(-i\hbar\psi' = p\psi\) gives \(\psi = Ae^{ikx}\), with \(k = p/\hbar\).
  2. Periodicity requires \(e^{ikL} = 1\), so \(k = 2\pi n/L\) with \(n\) any integer.
  3. Normalising over \([0,L]\): \(|A|^2L = 1\), so \(\psi_n = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt L}e^{2\pi inx/L}\).
  4. So \(p_n = \dfrac{2\pi n\hbar}{L}\), and \[ E_n = \frac{p_n^2}{2m} = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2n^2}{mL^2}. \]
  5. \(E_n\) depends on \(n^2\), so \(\psi_n\) and \(\psi_{-n}\) are degenerate for \(n \neq 0\): the two directions round the ring. The level \(n = 0\) is not degenerate.

Is \(\psi = \cos kx\) an eigenfunction of \(\hat p\)? Of \(\hat p^2\)? Write \(\psi\) as a superposition of momentum eigenstates, and state the possible results of a momentum measurement, with their relative probabilities.

Show solution
  1. \(\hat p\psi = i\hbar k\sin kx\), which is not a multiple of \(\psi\). So it is not an eigenfunction of \(\hat p\).

The simplest bound-state problem, and the source of most exam questions. Walls force the wave function to vanish, which quantises the energy.

Infinite square well, \(0 \leq x \leq L\)
\[ \phi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad E_n = \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2mL^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

Derivation

  1. Inside. \(V = 0\), so with \(k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2 \gt 0\): \[ \phi'' + k^2\phi = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx, \qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}. \]
  2. Outside. \(V = \infty\) rules out any normalisable solution with finite energy, so \(\phi \equiv 0\).
  3. Boundary conditions. Continuity of \(\phi\) at \(x = 0\) gives \(B = 0\). At \(x = L\), \(\sin kL = 0\), so \(k = n\pi/L\).
  4. Normalisation. \(1 = A_n^2\int_0^L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = A_n^2L/2\), so \(A_n = \sqrt{2/L}\).
Infinite walls. Only \(\phi\) is continuous here. Its derivative jumps at the walls. For finite potentials, both \(\phi\) and \(\phi'\) are continuous.

Averages in the \(n\)th state

\[ \langle x\rangle = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac L2, \qquad \langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\frac{2n\pi}{L^2}\int_0^L\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = 0. \]

Regardless of \(n\), the average position is the middle of the well, and the average momentum is zero. This does not mean the particle is at rest.

The same well, shifted to be symmetric. The energies do not change, but symmetry now sorts the states by parity.

Symmetric infinite well, \(-L \leq x \leq L\)
\[ \phi_n^-(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} \;\;\text{(odd)}, \qquad \phi_n^+(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\cos\frac{(2n+1)\pi x}{2L} \;\;\text{(even)} \]

Here \(n \geq 1\) for the odd states and \(n \geq 0\) for the even states.

Derivation

Requiring \(\phi = 0\) at both walls gives

\begin{equation} A\cos kL + B\sin kL = 0, \qquad A\cos kL - B\sin kL = 0. \tag{2.103} \end{equation}

Adding and subtracting gives \(A\cos kL = 0\) and \(B\sin kL = 0\). Both can only hold if one coefficient vanishes:

CaseConditionParityEnergy
\(A = 0\)\(\sin kL = 0\), \(\;kL = n\pi\)Odd\(\dfrac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2mL^2}\)
\(B = 0\)\(\cos kL = 0\), \(\;kL = \tfrac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}\)Even\(\dfrac{\hbar^2(2n+1)^2\pi^2}{8mL^2}\)

Normalisation can be read off from the earlier result by doubling the width of the well, which gives \(1/\sqrt L\).

Parity

Key fact. If \(V(x) = V(-x)\), then \(\hat H\) is unchanged by \(x \to -x\). So if \(\phi(x)\) solves the equation, so does \(\phi(-x)\). This means non-degenerate eigenfunctions are either even or odd.

With finite walls the particle leaks into the classically forbidden region, and the energies come from a transcendental equation.

Finite well: even bound states
\[ V = -V_0 \text{ for } |x| \leq L, \quad V = 0 \text{ outside}, \quad -V_0 \lt E \lt 0 \] \begin{equation} k = q\tan(qL) \tag{2.122} \end{equation} \[ k^2 = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}, \qquad q^2 = \frac{2m(E + V_0)}{\hbar^2}, \qquad k^2 + q^2 = \frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2} \]

The solution in each region

\begin{equation} \phi(x) = \begin{cases} Ae^{kx} & x \lt -L \\ B\cos qx & -L \leq x \leq L \\ Ae^{-kx} & x \gt L \end{cases} \tag{2.118} \end{equation}

The growing exponentials are discarded so that \(\phi\) is normalisable. Odd states use \(\sin qx\) in the middle.

Why \(\phi\) and \(\phi'\) are continuous

Integrate \(\phi'' = -\tfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\phi\) across a point \(x_0\), from \(x_0 - \epsilon\) to \(x_0 + \epsilon\). For finite \(V\), the integral tends to zero as \(\epsilon \to 0\), so \(\phi'\) has no jump. Continuity of \(\phi'\) then implies continuity of \(\phi\).

Matching and solving

At \(x = L\): \(Ae^{-kL} = B\cos qL\) and \(Ake^{-kL} = Bq\sin qL\). Dividing gives (2.122). In dimensionless form:

\begin{equation} kL = \sqrt{\frac{2mV_0L^2}{\hbar^2} - q^2L^2}, \qquad kL = qL\tan(qL). \tag{2.123} \end{equation}

Outside the well

Probability of being outside
\begin{equation} P_{\text{I,III}} = \frac{q^2}{(1 + kL)(k^2 + q^2)} = \frac{E + V_0}{V_0(1 + kL)} \gt 0 \tag{2.128} \end{equation}

This has no classical analogue. It tends to 0 as \(V_0 \to \infty\). The normalisation is \(|A|^2 = \dfrac{kq^2e^{2kL}}{(1 + kL)(k^2 + q^2)}\).

Other energies

EnergyBehaviour
\(E \lt -V_0\)No solution: real exponentials everywhere cannot be matched at both walls.
\(-V_0 \lt E \lt 0\)Discrete bound states, as above.
\(E \gt 0\)Continuum: free waves in every region, with \(q \gt k\) in the well, so a shorter wavelength there. No quantisation, and not normalisable.

Non-normalisable waves still carry a well-defined flow of probability. Comparing fluxes replaces normalisation in scattering problems.

Probability flux
\[ j(x) = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\frac{d\phi^*}{dx}\phi - \phi^*\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right) = \frac{1}{2m}\big[(\hat p\phi)^*\phi + \phi^*(\hat p\phi)\big] \] \[ \frac{d}{dt}P(a \leq x \leq b) = j(a) - j(b) \]

In words: rate of change of probability = flow in − flow out.

Derivation

  1. Differentiate \(P = \int_a^b\phi^*\phi\,dx\), and use \(\dot\phi = -\tfrac i\hbar\hat H\phi\): \[ \frac{dP}{dt} = \int_a^b\left[\frac i\hbar(\hat H\phi)^*\phi - \frac i\hbar\phi^*\hat H\phi\right]dx. \]
  2. With \(\hat H = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\tfrac{d^2}{dx^2} + V\), the real potential terms cancel: \[ \frac{dP}{dt} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int_a^b\big((\phi'')^*\phi - \phi^*\phi''\big)\,dx. \]
  3. Integrating by parts, the \(|\phi'|^2\) terms cancel, leaving the boundary terms \(j(a) - j(b)\).

\(\hat p\phi = \hbar k\phi\), so

\[ j = \frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2. \]

This is density times velocity: \(|A|^2\) is the probability density, and \(\hbar k/m\) is momentum over mass. It is like road traffic: cars passing per hour = cars per mile × miles per hour.

Free waves

For \(V = 0\) and \(E \gt 0\), with \(k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2\), the general solution is \(\psi = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}\). There is no boundary condition, so the spectrum \(E = \hbar^2k^2/2m\) is continuous. The flux is

\begin{equation} j = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\big(|A|^2 - |B|^2\big). \tag{2.147} \end{equation}

So \(Ae^{ikx}\) moves right and \(Be^{-ikx}\) moves left, and the cross terms cancel.

Unlike a classical particle, a quantum particle can reflect from a step it has enough energy to climb.

Step: \(E \gt V_1\)
\begin{equation} R = \left(\frac{k - k_1}{k + k_1}\right)^2, \qquad T = \frac{4kk_1}{(k + k_1)^2}, \qquad R + T = 1 \tag{2.160} \end{equation}

With \(k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2\) and \(k_1^2 = 2m(E - V_1)/\hbar^2\).

Case \(E \gt V_1\)

  1. With no wave coming in from the right: \[ \psi = \begin{cases} Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx} & x \lt 0 \\ Ce^{ik_1x} & x \gt 0. \end{cases} \]
  2. Continuity of \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) at \(x = 0\): \(A + B = C\) and \(ik(A - B) = ik_1C\).
  3. Solving: \[ C = \frac{2k}{k + k_1}A, \qquad B = \frac{k - k_1}{k + k_1}A. \]
  4. The fluxes are \(j_A = \tfrac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2\), \(j_B = -\tfrac{\hbar k}{m}|B|^2\), \(j_C = \tfrac{\hbar k_1}{m}|C|^2\). Define \(T = j_C/j_A\) and \(R = -j_B/j_A\).
Watch the \(k_1\). \(T\) is a ratio of fluxes, so it contains \(k_1/k\). It is not \(|C|^2/|A|^2\), because the wave moves at a different speed after the step.

Case \(E \lt V_1\)

Step: \(E \lt V_1\)
\[ \psi_{\text{II}} = Ce^{-qx}, \qquad q^2 = \frac{2m(V_1 - E)}{\hbar^2}, \qquad B = \frac{k - iq}{k + iq}A, \qquad R = 1, \quad T = 0 \]

Everything is reflected, but an exponential tail enters the classically forbidden region.

\(|B| = |A|\) because \(k - iq\) and \(k + iq\) are complex conjugates. The growing exponential is discarded to keep \(\psi\) finite at infinity. Continuity gives \(A + B = C\) and \(ik(A - B) = -qC\).

Give the step a finite width, and the decaying tail reaches the other side. Particles get through a barrier they classically cannot cross.

Tunnelling: \(E \lt V_1\)
\begin{equation} T = \frac{4k^2q^2}{(k^2 + q^2)^2\sinh^2(qL) + 4k^2q^2} \tag{2.183} \end{equation}

With \(k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2\) and \(q^2 = 2m(V_1 - E)/\hbar^2\). \(T\) is largest for a low, thin barrier.

Above the barrier: \(E \gt V_1\)
\[ T = \frac{4k^2k_1^2}{(k^2 - k_1^2)^2\sin^2(k_1L) + 4k^2k_1^2}, \qquad k_1^2 = \frac{2m(E - V_1)}{\hbar^2} \]

Part of the wave reflects even above the barrier. But \(T = 1\) when \(k_1L = n\pi\).

Setting up \(E \lt V_1\)

  1. The barrier is \(V = V_1\) on \(0 \lt x \lt L\). Now the growing exponential must be kept, since the region is finite: \[ \psi = \begin{cases} Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx} & x \lt 0 \\ Ce^{qx} + De^{-qx} & 0 \lt x \lt L \\ Fe^{ikx} & x \gt L. \end{cases} \]
  2. Matching at \(x = L\) gives \[ C = \frac{Fe^{ikL}(q + ik)e^{-qL}}{2q}, \qquad D = \frac{Fe^{ikL}(q - ik)e^{qL}}{2q}. \]
  3. Matching at \(x = 0\), then substituting: \[ A = \frac{Fe^{ikL}}{2ikq}\big[(k^2 - q^2)\sinh qL + 2ikq\cosh qL\big], \quad B = \frac{Fe^{ikL}}{2ikq}(k^2 + q^2)\sinh qL. \]
  4. Both sides have momentum \(\hbar k\), so \(T = |F|^2/|A|^2\) and \(R = |B|^2/|A|^2\). The form (2.183) uses \((k^2 - q^2)^2 + 4k^2q^2 = (k^2 + q^2)^2\), and clearly \(T + R = 1\).
Application\(\alpha\)-decay

In a nucleus, the short-range nuclear force binds protons and neutrons, while the electrostatic force is repulsive. Together they form a potential barrier. \(\alpha\)-particles tunnel out through it, and the \(\alpha\)-decay rate matches the tunnelling probability one can estimate.

Measuring changes a system, so the order of measurements matters. The commutator measures exactly how much.

Canonical commutation relation
\begin{equation} [\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar \tag{3.7} \end{equation}

In 3D, \([x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}\). All other position–momentum commutators vanish.

Definitions and basic rules

\begin{equation} [A,B] = AB - BA, \qquad [A,A] = 0, \qquad [A,B] = -[B,A]. \tag{3.1} \end{equation}

An operator is anti-Hermitian if \(\langle\phi|B\psi\rangle = -\langle B\phi|\psi\rangle\). If \(A\) is Hermitian, then \(iA\) is anti-Hermitian.

Key result. If \(A\) and \(B\) are Hermitian, then \([A,B]\) is anti-Hermitian. So we can write \([A,B] = iC\), with \(C\) Hermitian.

Evaluating a commutator

Because \(\hat p\) is a differential operator, act on a test function:

\[ [\hat x, \hat p]f = x(-i\hbar)\frac{df}{dx} + i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}(xf) = -i\hbar x\frac{df}{dx} + i\hbar f + i\hbar x\frac{df}{dx} = i\hbar f. \]

The right side of (3.7) contains the unit operator, which is usually not written out.

The general inequality

For Hermitian \(A, B\), let \(\phi = (A + i\lambda B)\psi\) with \(\lambda\) real. Then

\begin{equation} \langle\phi|\phi\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle - \lambda\langle C\rangle + \lambda^2\langle B^2\rangle \geq 0. \tag{3.9} \end{equation}

A quadratic in \(\lambda\) that is never negative cannot have two distinct real roots, so its discriminant gives

\begin{equation} \langle A^2\rangle\langle B^2\rangle \geq \tfrac14\langle C\rangle^2. \tag{3.11} \end{equation}

Not a limit on instruments: position and momentum cannot both be sharp, because of how the operators fail to commute.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle
\begin{equation} \Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \frac\hbar2 \tag{3.17} \end{equation}

with \((\Delta A)^2 = \langle(A - \langle A\rangle)^2\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2\).

Derivation

  1. Shift the operators: \(\tilde x = \hat x - \langle x\rangle\) and \(\tilde p = \hat p - \langle p\rangle\). The averages are numbers, so \([\tilde x, \tilde p] = [\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar\), meaning \(C = \hbar\).
  2. Applying (3.11): \(\langle\tilde x^2\rangle\langle\tilde p^2\rangle \geq \tfrac14\hbar^2\).
  3. By definition this is \((\Delta x)^2(\Delta p)^2 \geq \tfrac14\hbar^2\).

Consequences

  • In the classical limit \(\hbar \to 0\), there is no such uncertainty.
  • If operators commute, the observables may be measured precisely together. For example, \([\hat p_x, \hat y] = 0\).
  • In three dimensions, the bound applies in each direction separately.
  • A plane wave \(e^{ikx}\) has exact momentum \(\hbar k\), but \(|\psi|^2 = 1\) everywhere, so \(\Delta x = \infty\).
  • If \(\Delta x \sim L\), then \(\Delta p \sim 1/L\).
Energy–time uncertainty
\begin{equation} \Delta E\,\Delta t \geq \frac\hbar2 \tag{3.18} \end{equation}

This allows energy to be "borrowed" for a short time, making tunnelling and vacuum fluctuations possible.

Where it comes from

To measure a position, photons must scatter off the particle, transferring an uncertain momentum. A sharper position measurement needs shorter wavelengths or more photons, which disturbs the momentum more.

The Gaussian is the most classical state possible: it exactly saturates the uncertainty bound.

Gaussian integrals
\[ I_n(a) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^ne^{-ax^2}\,dx, \qquad I_0 = \sqrt{\frac\pi a}, \qquad I_{2n+1} = 0, \qquad I_{2n+2} = -\frac{dI_{2n}}{da} \]

So \(I_2 = \dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2a^{3/2}}\) and \(I_4 = \dfrac{3\sqrt\pi}{4a^{5/2}}\). Odd powers vanish by symmetry.

Gaussian state saturates the bound
\[ \psi(x) = \left(\frac a\pi\right)^{1/4}e^{-\frac12ax^2}, \qquad (\Delta x)^2 = \frac{1}{2a}, \quad (\Delta p)^2 = \frac{a\hbar^2}{2}, \quad \Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac\hbar2 \]

The calculation

  1. Normalisation. \(|A|^2I_0(a) = 1\), so \(A = (a/\pi)^{1/4}\).
  2. Position. \(\langle x\rangle = 0\) since the integrand is odd, and \[ \langle x^2\rangle = |A|^2I_2(a) = \sqrt{\frac a\pi}\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2a^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{2a}. \]
  3. Momentum. \(\hat p\psi = i\hbar ax\psi\), so \(\langle p\rangle = 0\). Using Hermiticity, \(\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \langle\hat p\psi|\hat p\psi\rangle\), which is simpler: \[ \langle\hat p^2\rangle = \hbar^2a^2|A|^2I_2(a) = \frac{a\hbar^2}{2}. \]

Fourier transform

Completing the square gives another Gaussian:

\begin{equation} \tilde f(k) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ax^2}e^{ikx}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac\pi a}\,e^{-k^2/4a}. \tag{3.38} \end{equation}

A superposition of plane waves can localise a particle. A Gaussian packet is the closest QM gets to a classical particle.

Wave packet
\begin{equation} \psi(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty C(k)\,e^{ikx - i\omega(k)t}\,dk, \qquad \omega(k) = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m} \tag{3.40} \end{equation}

A Gaussian packet has \(C(k) = Ae^{-\alpha^2(k - k_0)^2}\).

A free particle has solutions \(\psi_k = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}\) with \(\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m\), which are not normalisable. Superposing them gives a normalisable state, but with no definite momentum or energy, so it changes shape with time. A wave packet that is Gaussian initially stays Gaussian, with a complex, time-dependent width.

From a wave function to a potential

If \(\psi = Ae^{-f(x)}\) is an eigenstate with energy \(E\), then \(\psi'' = (f'^2 - f'')\psi\), and the Schrödinger equation gives

\[ V(x) = E + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big(f'^2 - f''\big). \]
